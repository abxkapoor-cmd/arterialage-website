The stakes

The root cause

Medicine was built for one kind of body. Yours may not be it.

Standard oscillometric BP monitors — used in virtually every GP surgery in the UK — were calibrated against a single baseline demographic: white males in their 60s. In patients with different arterial geometries and stiffness, these devices systematically misread. A patient can appear to have a normal blood pressure while their arteries tell a completely different story.

A pathway designed for the few

Echocardiography misses HFpEF by design

It screens for reduced ejection fraction. And it is the backlogged bottleneck: patients wait an average of 27 days between their first echocardiogram and their first heart failure admission.

The gold standard is out of reach

Invasive exercise cardiac catheterisation, available at fewer than ten designated UK centres.

The main symptom doesn't trigger referral

Vague breathlessness rarely escalates. GPs have no tool to separate the "worried well" from the quietly dying.

No home-based arterial monitoring exists

Nothing in any standard care pathway measures arterial stiffness ambulantly.

The insight

The warning isn't weight or blood pressure. It's arterial stiffness.

HFpEF is increasingly understood as a disease of the vasculature, not only the heart muscle. As the large arteries stiffen with vascular ageing, the workload on the heart rises, ventricular–arterial coupling deteriorates, and the heart's ability to fill declines.

The evidence

Two questions an investor asks. Both now have published answers.

Does the signal relate to HFpEF?

27 studies, 6,626 patients

A meta-analysis found pulse wave velocity measures correlate significantly with E/e′ and E/A — the echocardiographic markers of diastolic dysfunction essential for diagnosing HFpEF. Higher aortic PWV in HFpEF is associated with increased hospitalisation and cardiovascular mortality.

Does finding it earlier change anything?

It didn't. Now it does.

HFpEF used to have no disease-modifying treatment, so earlier detection changed nothing. SGLT2 inhibitors ended that: across DELIVER and EMPEROR-Preserved (12,251 participants), first hospitalisation for heart failure fell with a hazard ratio of 0.74 — roughly a 26% relative reduction — and they are now guideline-recommended for HFpEF.

Pre-HFpEF

An earlier phenotype to catch

Pulse wave velocity may identify at-risk intermediate phenotypes — so-called "pre-HFpEF" — before overt disease appears. That is a screening population no existing tool addresses.

The solution

A wearable that watches your arteries — not just your pulse.

A passive 14-day home wearable that measures arterial stiffness by timing the pulse between two body sites, then applies a machine-learning model to classify HFpEF risk from six physiological features. No cuff, no needle, no clinic visit.

The product

Three parts: one hub, two vascular vantage points.

A chest hub carrying the processor, battery and radio, plus two optical PPG sensors — one at the wrist, one at the earlobe. Both share a hardware exposure clock, so the difference in pulse arrival between the two sites can be timed to sub-millisecond precision. That difference is pulse transit time, and from it, pulse wave velocity.

The pulse leaves the heart and travels two arterial paths of unequal length. It reaches the earlobe first and the wrist second; Δt is that difference. Timing comes from the shared clock across two optical sensors — there is no ECG channel in this device, and no ECG signal is consumed, duplicated or replaced.

Chest hub

Where the model runs

Host processor, Bluetooth and battery. The six-feature HFpEF risk classifier runs on board, so the patient wears a screening device rather than a data logger.

Wrist pod

Reflectance mode

Light goes into the tissue and comes back out the same side. Three wavelengths are used — 940 nm infrared penetrates deepest and is least affected by melanin.

Earlobe clip

Transmission mode

Light passes straight through the lobe, giving a cleaner pulse waveform at the site the pulse reaches first. No cuff, no needle, no electrodes.

Four mechanisms

Where the savings actually come from.

01 — Upstream diagnosis

A passive home screen flags likely HFpEF patients in primary care, so a proportion are diagnosed and treated before reaching the front door. Each genuinely avoided admission banks £738–£1,107.

02 — Protecting echo capacity

Our six-feature risk score raises pre-test probability, so scarce echocardiography goes to the patients most likely to actually have HFpEF. That cuts negative-yield referrals against a 27-day queue.

03 — Detecting paroxysmal AF

Atrial fibrillation is a common HFpEF comorbidity, detectable from pulse rhythm over a two-week window without an ECG. Catching it enables anticoagulation that prevents expensive stroke admissions.

04 — Reducing readmissions

The baseline is poor: 22.3% of heart failure patients are readmitted within 30 days, 33.3% within 60 and 40.2% within 90. Earlier guideline-directed therapy attacks this directly.

Illustrative scale Modelled — not a forecast
Of 76,000–95,000 new HFpEF cases a year, around 80% are currently diagnosed late. If screening shifted 10% of those upstream and each avoided one admission: roughly 6,000–7,600 admissions avoided per year, worth ~£4.5m–£8.4m cash-releasing. The 10% shift is the one assumption in this chain — and it is precisely what our first NHS pilot exists to measure.

Defensibility

What is actually hard to copy.

Architecture

Two synchronised sites

Pulse transit time requires two sites sharing a hardware clock. A single-site patch cannot produce it — that is an architectural difference, not a feature that can be switched on.

Model

Six-feature risk classifier

Combining all six features into a single HFpEF risk output is the core intellectual property, and the reason we can hold a Zio-level price rather than a commodity-sensor price.

Evidence

Clinical dataset and Class IIa barrier

Consumer wearables do single-site rhythm detection, not two-site pulse wave velocity, and are not regulated for HFpEF screening. Our moat is clinical evidence plus regulatory clearance — a time-and-money barrier.

Why per-episode beats a one-off sale

It matches how the NHS buys

Subscription fees are operational rather than capital expenditure — simplified budgeting, quicker approval, faster access. The NHS is chronically capital-constrained, so this is often the difference between "approved next year" and "start next month".

Each customer compounds

A one-off sale requires finding a new buyer every time. An episode base grows without new logos.

The market pays for recurring revenue

The razor/blade model earns 3–5 additional EBITDA turns relative to capital-equipment-heavy peers. Where 50–70% of revenue is consumables, service or software tied to the device, buyers treat the company as a platform.

The honest caveat

A managed service front-loads working capital, because we fund each deployed device before collecting episode fees. Capital sales to private clinics are useful for near-term cash.

Stage, stated plainly

What we have proved, and what we have not.

Proved — the physiology

Arterial stiffness is an early vascular signal of the same disease process, correlated with the echo markers used to diagnose HFpEF, and measurable non-invasively.

Open — sub-millisecond two-site timing

Our first validation milestone. The timing performance depends on two optical front ends sharing a hardware exposure clock. The approach is not novel physics, so the risk is execution, not invention.

Design-mitigated — skin tone

Three wavelengths mitigate melanin effects: 940 nm infrared penetrates deepest and is least affected. Monk-scale cross-skin-tone validation is mandated in our own specification — the architecture is the mitigation, the study is the proof.

Open — reprocessing and device longevity

Validated infection-control cleaning between patients, and evidence that repeat cycles do not degrade the optical window.
